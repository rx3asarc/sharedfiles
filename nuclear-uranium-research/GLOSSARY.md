# GLOSSARY — Plain English

Every term used in this pack, defined without jargon. If a term is missing, it is a gap —
add it.

**Rule for this file:** if a definition needs another undefined term to make sense, the
definition is broken. Rewrite it.

---

## A — Investing structure

### Core
The big, boring middle of a portfolio. Usually one broad index fund holding thousands of
companies. **Job: don't lose the base.** Typically 75–90% of capital.

*Not a product — a role.* Anything can be the core if its job is stability.

### Satellite
Small positions that sit alongside the core, deliberately taking more risk.
**Job: try to make a lot.** Typically 10–25% of capital, in 1–5 holdings.

The word is just an orbit metaphor — small thing circling a big thing.

### Core-satellite
The whole arrangement: most money safe, little bit risky. The reason it exists is that the
two parts do different jobs and can't be judged by the same standard.

### Position
One holding. If you own €2,000 of Cameco, that's one position.

### Position sizing
How much you put in each thing. **The most underrated decision in investing** — it decides
how much damage a mistake does.

### Allocation
How your money is split up. "80% core / 20% satellite" is an allocation.

### Concentration
Having a lot in one thing. Concentrated = few positions, each big. Diversified = many
positions, each small.

### Diversification
Spreading money so no single thing can ruin you. **Not the same as owning many names** —
26 stocks in one theme can be one bet wearing 26 hats.

### Correlation
Whether things move together. High correlation = they rise and fall at the same time, so
owning both adds no safety. **This is why the 26-name basket is really ~4 bets.**

### Drawdown
The fall from a peak to a bottom, measured in %. A stock going €100 → €40 is a 60% drawdown.

### Bear market / bull market
Sustained falling / rising market. Bear = pessimism, bull = optimism. Nothing more precise.

### Liquidity
How easily you can buy or sell without moving the price. High liquidity = lots of trading,
you can get in and out. **Low liquidity = you can't exit without pushing the price down.**

### Float
The shares actually available to trade (excluding locked-up insider holdings). Low float =
price moves violently on small trades.

### Short interest
How much of a stock has been borrowed and sold by people betting it falls. High short
interest = the price is partly driven by positioning, not fundamentals.

## B — Fund wrappers (the packaging your money sits in)

### The core problem with all of this
You rarely buy a company directly. You buy it *through* something — a fund, an ETF, an
account. **That packaging has its own rules, costs, and legal status.** Two people holding
the same stock can have completely different outcomes because of the wrapper.

### ETF
Exchange-Traded Fund. A basket of many stocks that trades like a single stock. Cheap, easy.

### UCITS
The European legal standard for funds. **If something is UCITS, it's generally sellable to
European retail investors** and subject to EU rules on diversification and disclosure.

### PRIIPs
EU rule requiring a plain-language document ("KID") explaining a product's risks.
**Practical effect: most US-domiciled ETFs don't produce one, so you can't legally buy them
from Austria.** That's why URA (US) is blocked but URNU (UCITS) works.

### Domicile
The country a fund is legally based in. **A "US-domiciled" ETF is blocked for you even if it
tracks a European index.** Domicile decides the rules, not the contents.

### ISIN
A 12-character international ID code for a security. `IE000NDWFGA5` is URNU. Like a barcode
for financial products.

### Ticker
The short code you type to trade something. `CCJ` = Cameco, `URA` = Global X Uranium ETF.

### TER
Total Expense Ratio. The fund's annual fee, in %. **0.2% vs 0.6% sounds trivial and isn't** —
over 20 years it's a real amount of money.

### Accumulating vs distributing
**Accumulating:** the fund reinvests dividends internally; you get no cash, the price rises.
**Distributing:** you receive the dividends as cash.

*This matters enormously in Austria* — see Meldefonds below.

## C — Austrian tax terms

⚠️ **Not tax advice.** Verify with a Steuerberater before acting on any of this.

### KESt (Kapitalertragsteuer)
Austrian capital gains tax. **27.5%** on investment gains and dividends. Usually deducted at
source by your broker, so you often don't file it yourself.

### Meldefonds
The critical one. Austria distinguishes between:
- **Meldefonds** ("reporting fund") — the fund has an Austrian tax representative and
  publishes the data the tax office needs. Taxed normally.
- **Non-reporting funds (Schwarzfonds / "black funds")** — no Austrian representation.
  **Taxed punitively**, and the tax office may assume a flat deemed profit regardless of
  what you actually earned.

**Practical consequence: a fund can be perfectly legal to buy and still be a bad idea
because of its tax status.** Always check.

### ausschüttungsgleiche Erträge
"Deemed distributions." Austria taxes accumulating funds **annually on the gains they didn't
pay out.** So an accumulating fund creates a tax bill each year even though you received no
cash — you may need to find the money elsewhere to pay it.

**This is the single most important Austrian quirk for a small investor.** It's why the
"just buy an accumulating ETF" advice from US sources can go wrong here.

### Verlustausgleich
Loss offsetting. Rules on using losses to reduce tax on gains. Worth asking an advisor.

## D — Reading a company

### Market cap
Share price × number of shares = what the whole company is worth on paper. "A €6.74bn
company" means market cap, not cash.

### Revenue
Money coming in from selling things. **Not profit** — it's the top line.

### Profitability
Whether money is left after costs. A company can have huge revenue and lose money.

### Gross margin
Revenue minus direct cost of making the product, as a %. Higher = more pricing power. A
margin going from −74% to +17% is a real turnaround; 60% is a software company.

### Cash burn
How fast a company spends money it isn't earning. **The core diagnostic of this whole
project.** High burn + no revenue = permanently dependent on selling shares.

### Going concern
An auditor's formal warning that there's **substantial doubt the company can survive the
next 12 months.** This is the strongest possible signal of danger. Not a rumour — a legal
statement in the accounts.

### Backlog
Value of signed contracts not yet delivered or paid. **A promise about the future, not money
today.** The finding here: backlog did *not* predict returns; profit did.

### Offtake agreement
A contract to buy a company's future output. Guarantees demand if the buyer is good for it.

### Capex
Capital expenditure — money spent on buildings, machines, mines. Capex-heavy businesses need
constant cash and therefore constant fundraising.

### Dilution
When a company issues new shares, existing shareholders own a smaller slice. **Your share of
the company shrinks even though you did nothing.** It's how you get quietly robbed by a
company that can't fund itself.

### ATM (At-The-Market) offering
A facility letting a company drip-sell new shares into the market whenever it wants.
Not a one-off event — **an ongoing mechanism.** Oklo raised $1.9bn this way and filed for
$1bn more while holding $3.0bn in cash.

### Equity issuance
Selling new shares to raise money. **The central test in this pack:**
- Issuing occasionally, into strength, on good terms → fine, even a confidence signal
- Issuing to survive → ruinous
- Issuing as the operating model → structurally corrosive

### Reverse split
Combining shares to lift the price (e.g. 1-for-15 means 15 old shares become 1).
**Cosmetic, not a recovery** — it's often a sign things went badly.

### Going-concern flag vs. repaired balance sheet
A company can fix the *warning* without fixing the *business.* Velo3D repaired its balance
sheet while the stock halved.

## E — Valuation and returns

### Valuation
What the market is willing to pay for a company. Usually compared to revenue or profit.

### P/NAV
Price to Net Asset Value. For holding companies: are you paying more than the assets are
worth? 2.4x → 1.5x means the market got less generous — the assets didn't change, the
enthusiasm did.

### Compounding
Gains earning gains. The reason time matters more than cleverness. A small sum at 7% for 20
years → €42,567 without adding a cent.

### Contributions
Money you add over time. **In a 20-year projection, contributions were ~79% of a
20-year outcome.** The single most powerful variable, and the one fully under your control.

### Real vs nominal
**Nominal** = the number on the page. **Real** = after inflation. €42,567 in 20 years buys
less than €42,567 today.

### Expected value
The average outcome if you repeated a bet many times. **A bet can have a positive expected
value and still be a bad idea** — if the losing outcomes would ruin you, the average is
irrelevant.

### Risk of ruin
The chance of losing enough that you can't recover. **The reason not to put 100% of capital
in one theme** — it beats expected value when the base is irreplaceable.

### Mark-to-market
Valuing a holding at its current market price. A €50bn position isn't €50bn until sold —
lock-up rules and thin markets can make the paper number unrealisable. SpaceX's ~$50bn
mark is mark-to-market, not cash.

### Round trip
Buying and then selling. "2.36% round trip" = total cost of getting in and back out.

### Spread
The gap between two prices. In the neocloud case: the gap between rental revenue and
financing + depreciation costs.

### Carry trade
Borrowing cheaply to buy something yielding more, and pocketing the difference. **Works
until it doesn't** — a small change in either rate flips a gain into a loss, and the
borrowing still has to be repaid.

## F — Funds, filings, and market terms

### 13F
A quarterly US filing where large investment managers list their US stock holdings.
**Two critical caveats:**
1. It shows **gross exposure, not assets under management.** A leveraged fund's filing can
   be many times its actual money. **Never quote a 13F as AUM.**
2. It's **~45 days stale** and covers only long US equities — no shorts, no private
   companies.

### AUM
Assets Under Management — the money a fund actually runs. Different from 13F value.

### ADV
Average Daily Volume — how much of a stock trades on a normal day. **The number that decides
whether you can exit.** A €2,000 position in something trading $17,500/day is 12% of the
tape.

### Pink Sheets / OTC
Unofficial US marketplaces for small companies. Barely regulated, extremely thin trading.
MAXQF trades there.

### ETF overlap
When two different funds hold the same stocks. **Two ETFs can look like diversification and
be the same bet** — CCJ, UEC, DNN and UUUU sit in both URA and URNM.

### Index fund
A fund that just copies a market index instead of picking stocks. Cheap, no human judgement,
no single-name risk.

### Rebalancing
Periodically resetting your split back to target (e.g. back to 80/20 after a good run).
Mechanical discipline instead of emotion.

## G — Uranium / nuclear (specific to this basket)

### U3O8
The uranium oxide that gets traded. "Yellowcake."

### Spot price
The price for immediate delivery. Volatile, sentiment-driven.

### Term price
The price for delivery years out, set by long-term contracts. **Slower and more meaningful
— it's where producers actually sell.** The project's open question: spot rose to meet term,
but the miners fell anyway.

### Enrichment
Raising the concentration of U-235 in uranium so it can be used as fuel. Centrus is the only
Western company doing it at scale.

### HALEU
High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium. The fuel advanced reactors need. **Almost nobody makes it** —
which is why Centrus is structurally important and why the Centrus↔Oklo link matters.

### LEU
Low-Enriched Uranium. Standard reactor fuel.

### SMR
Small Modular Reactor. Factory-built nuclear reactor instead of a huge bespoke plant.

### Criticality
The moment a reactor sustains a controlled nuclear chain reaction. **The June document
treated Oklo's as a landmark catalyst. Five reactors achieved it in one summer** — see the
notes on why a commoditised milestone isn't a catalyst, and why criticality isn't revenue.

### ISR (In-Situ Recovery)
Mining uranium by pumping fluids underground rather than digging. Cheaper and faster to
start. UEC's Burke Hollow was the first new US ISR mine in over a decade.

### FAST-41
A US law giving major infrastructure projects a **fixed, enforceable federal permitting
timetable.** Relevant to Trilogy Metals' Arctic project.

### DPA (Defense Production Act)
Law letting the US government direct industrial production for national security. Trilogy's
deal was contingent on its reauthorization.

### FOCI
Foreign Ownership, Control or Influence — a US security review of who owns a defence-critical
company. Can block or delay deals.

### LOI
Letter of Intent. A statement that parties *intend* to make a deal. **Not a binding contract.**
Trilogy's government stake sat as an LOI for months.

## H — The framework's own vocabulary

### Bottleneck
Something the buildout can't proceed without. Only useful if it can't be replicated in time.

### Class A / B / C constraint
The project's ranking of how hard a bottleneck is to solve:
- **A** — solvable with money (weeks to months)
- **B** — solvable with time (years of learning)
- **C** — solvable only with permission (laws, licences, land rights)

**Rent durability is inverse to solvability.** Class C is where durable profit lives.

### Rent
Profit above the cost of capital, earned because something can't be copied.

### Non-replicable
Can't be reproduced on the timescale that matters. The test for whether rent exists.

### Spot-priced vs contract-priced
Whether the thing you own sells at a market price that changes daily, or under a contract
that fixes the terms. **Contract-priced = bounded and predictable. Spot-priced = unlimited
both directions, and leverage turns it into ruin.**

### Self-funding
Generates enough cash to run itself without selling shares.
**The observable fingerprint of rent.**

### Catalyst
An expected event that will change a company's prospects. **Has a date. Gets consumed.**

### Class-4 latent catalyst
The project's term for an **unpriced, dated event sitting under an existing holding** — the
only genuinely free thing in the system. Example: Cameco's 49% of Westinghouse, which filed a draft IPO registration in July 2026.

### Commoditised catalyst
An event *everyone* achieves at once, so it stops being information. Five reactors reaching
criticality in one summer.

### Owner-side constraint
A limit on **you**, not the asset — whether you can legally buy it, whether the market can
absorb your size, whether you have the time for the thesis to work. A perfect asset you
can't own is worth zero.

### Wrapper
The legal container your money sits in. **Solves access, not selection** — buying URNU makes
the uranium theme purchasable and also imports Oklo at 8.43%.
